A good idea is a starting point. Building a useful product also depends on understanding users, making practical delivery decisions and learning from evidence. These five mistakes can create avoidable work before a first launch.

## 1. Choosing a team without checking project fit

Review relevant work and ask who will actually deliver your project. Discuss communication, availability, ownership and handover before comparing prices. Freelancers, agencies and internal teams can all be effective when their capabilities fit the work.

Use the same questions for each candidate. Our [development partner guide](/posts/finding-the-best-software-outsourcing-candidate-for-your-project) provides a starting point.

## 2. Treating assumptions as evidence of demand

Describe the problem and talk to people who experience it. Ask how they solve it now, what the current approach costs them and why they would change. A compliment about an idea is not the same as a commitment to use or pay for a product.

Look at competing solutions, including spreadsheets, manual work and doing nothing. These are part of the decision your customer will make.

## 3. Building before testing the difficult decisions

A sketch, prototype or small technical experiment can answer a specific uncertainty. Use the cheapest meaningful test: a clickable flow for usability, or a narrow integration experiment for data access.

Not every project needs a polished prototype. The point is to learn before committing to the parts that will be expensive to change.

## 4. Putting every idea into the first release

An MVP is a minimum viable product: it should deliver a useful outcome and create an opportunity to learn. Decide which user journey must work and postpone features that do not support it.

For a booking service, completing a reservation may matter more than an advanced loyalty scheme. Agree what happens when a reservation fails as well as when it succeeds.

## 5. Collecting feedback without making decisions

Observe how people use the product, record problems and compare requests with the product's purpose. User feedback is valuable, but not every requested feature should be built.

Choose changes by their likely value and cost, then review whether they helped. Keep operational work, support and maintenance in the plan too.
